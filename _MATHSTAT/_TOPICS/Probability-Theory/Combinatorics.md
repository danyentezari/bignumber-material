# Combinatorics

Combinatorics is the mathematics of counting things efficiently. In combinatorics, we have techniques for determining the number of possible outcomes of experiments without direct enumeration (i.e, without manually counting). In this section, we will look at some of these techniques which have applications in probability distributions and their functions.

Multiplication principle. A sequence of choices has a total number of outcomes equal to the product of the numbers of available choices at each stage. This principle is used to count compound outcomes in multistage probability experiments.

Addition principle. Mutually exclusive collections of outcomes can be counted by adding their individual numbers of outcomes. This principle is used to divide complicated probability spaces into disjoint cases and count their union.

Permutation. A permutation counts selections in which the ordering of selected objects matters. This principle is used to count ordered arrangements, rankings, sequences, and sampling outcomes without replacement.

Combination. A combination counts selections in which only the selected objects matter and their ordering does not. This principle is used to count subsets, samples, committees, and unordered collections of outcomes.

Binomial coefficient. The binomial coefficient gives the number of ways to select a fixed number of objects from a finite collection without replacement and without regard to order. This principle is used to count possible success configurations in binomial probability models.

## Problems

- Obtain $|\Omega|$ for a finite sample space of equally likely outcomes.
- Obtain $P(A)=|A|/|\Omega|$ on a finite uniform probability space.
- Count ordered $k$-tuples chosen from $n$ objects without replacement.
- Count unordered $k$-subsets chosen from $n$ objects.
- Count outcomes of sampling with replacement and of sampling without replacement.
- Count sequences with $k$ successes in $n$ Bernoulli trials.
