# Law of Large Numbers

The *law of large numbers* states that as the size of the sample increases, the mean of the sample will approach the mean of its population.

## Problems

- Establish $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$ for independent identically distributed random variables with finite mean.
- Establish $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}} \mu$ for independent identically distributed integrable random variables.
- Obtain $S_n/n \xrightarrow{P} 0$ for independent mean-zero summands under weak-law conditions.
- Obtain $S_n/n \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}} 0$ for independent identically distributed mean-zero integrable summands.
- Obtain $n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n 1_{\{X_i\in A\}} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}} P(X\in A)$.
- Obtain $n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}} E[g(X)]$ when $g(X)$ is integrable.
- Identify $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$ as the weak-law conclusion and $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}} \mu$ as the strong-law conclusion.

Weak law of large numbers. For independent identically distributed random variables with finite mean, the sample mean converges in probability to the common mean. This principle is used to establish that large sample averages become arbitrarily close to the population mean with probability approaching one.

Strong law of large numbers. Under standard independence and integrability conditions, the sample mean converges almost surely to the common mean. This principle is used to establish pathwise convergence of sample averages for almost every infinite sequence of observations.

Convergence in probability. A sequence converges in probability when the probability of differing from its limit by more than any fixed positive amount tends to zero. This principle is used to express the mode of convergence established by the weak law.

Almost-sure convergence. A sequence converges almost surely when convergence occurs for all outcomes except a set having probability zero. This principle is used to express the stronger mode of convergence established by the strong law.

Independence. Independence prevents persistent dependence among successive summands in the classical laws of large numbers. This principle is used to control accumulated fluctuations so that averaging suppresses random variation.

Finite mean condition. Integrability ensures that the common expectation exists and provides the limiting value of the sample mean. This principle is used to identify the constant toward which averages converge under the standard strong law.

Sample-mean stabilization. For identically distributed integrable independent variables, repeated averaging causes random fluctuations to vanish asymptotically around the common expectation. This principle is used to connect probabilistic expectations with limiting empirical averages.
