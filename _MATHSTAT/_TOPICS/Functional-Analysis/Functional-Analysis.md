# Functional Analysis

Functional analysis is the study of function spaces and the operators acting on function spaces. In functional analysis, functions are viewed as points in a set.

This idea that functions are treated as points in a set is relevant because this allows us to, in probability theory, guarantee that limits and projections of random variables exist within the sets of functions where probability calculations occur.

#### *Example 1.*

Let

$$
X_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{-k}R_k
$$

where

- $R_k$ are independent random variables
- $E[R_k]=0$
- $E[R_k^2]=1$
- $2^{-k}$ is the coefficient weighting the $k$th random variable
- $k$ is the index of the random variables in the sequence

Because $L^2$ is complete,

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{L^2}X.
$$

Here, $X\in L^2$ and $\xrightarrow{L^2}$ denotes convergence in $L^2$.

Because $L^2(\sigma(R_1))$ is a closed subspace,

$$
E[X\mid R_1]
=
\frac12R_1.
$$

Here, $\sigma(R_1)$ is the sigma-algebra generated by $R_1$.

The conditional expectation exists as the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto $L^2(\sigma(R_1))$ [2, 3].

### *Lemma. $(X_n)$ is Cauchy in $L^2$.*

Let

$$
\begin{aligned}
X_j
&=
\sum_{k=1}^{j}\left(\frac12\right)^kR_k
=
2^{-1}R_1+2^{-2}R_2+\cdots+2^{-j}R_j
\\[0.5em]
X_m-X_n
&=
\left(2^{-1}R_1+\cdots+2^{-n}R_n+\cdots+2^{-m}R_m\right)
-\left(2^{-1}R_1+\cdots+2^{-n}R_n\right)
\\
&=
2^{-(n+1)}R_{n+1}+\cdots+2^{-m}R_m
=
\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}2^{-k}R_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

where $m>n$


Therefore, $\|X_m-X_n\|_2^2=E[(X_m-X_n)^2]$.


Expanding, $E[(X_m-X_n)^2]=\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}4^{-k}E[R_k^2]$.


The cross terms vanish because $R_k$ are independent and $E[R_k]=0$.


Since $E[R_k^2]=1$, $\|X_m-X_n\|_2^2=\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}4^{-k}$.


Since $\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}4^{-k}\to0$, $\|X_m-X_n\|_2\to0$.


Therefore, $(X_n)$ is Cauchy in $L^2$.

#### *Example 2.*
Let

$$
X=R_1+R_2
$$

where

- $R_1,R_2$ are independent random variables
- $E[R_2]=0$
- only $R_1$ is known

Then $E[X\mid R_1]=R_1$, where $R_1$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto $L^2(\sigma(R_1))$.

The projection theorem gives $E[(X-R_1)^2]=\min_{Z\in L^2(\sigma(R_1))}E[(X-Z)^2]$, where $Z\in L^2(\sigma(R_1))$.

Thus $R_1$ is the unique best mean-square predictor of $X$ [2, 3].
