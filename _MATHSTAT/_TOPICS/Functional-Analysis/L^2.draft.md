# $L^2$

A Hilbert space that is used to treat square-integrable random variables as vectors with inner product equal to expectation of the product, where a square-integrable random variable is a measurable function with finite second moment, and where a Hilbert space is a complete inner product space.

Note: Also called the space of square-integrable random variables.

Definition of $L^2$. On a probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$, the space $L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ consists of (equivalence classes of) real random variables with finite second moment. Two random variables are identified when they agree almost surely. This principle is used to assemble square-integrable random variables into a vector space.

The space is

$$
L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
=
\bigl\{X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}:
X\text{ is }\mathcal{F}\text{-measurable and }
E[X^2]<\infty\bigr\}/\sim,
$$

where $X\sim Y$ if and only if $P(X=Y)=1$.

where

- $\Omega$ is the sample space.
- $\mathcal{F}$ is the $\sigma$-algebra of events.
- $P$ is the probability measure.
- $X$ is a real random variable.
- $E$ is expectation.
- $\sim$ is almost-sure equality.

Gut defines the $L^p$ norm of a random variable with a finite moment of order $p$ by $\|X\|_p=(E|X|^p)^{1/p}$. Klenke calls $X$ square integrable when $X\in L^2(P)$ and passes to equivalence classes of functions that agree almost everywhere. Ash and Doléans-Dade obtain $L^p$ by identifying functions that agree almost everywhere.

Inner product and norm. The pairing $E[XY]$ is an inner product on $L^2$. The induced norm is the square root of the second moment. This principle is used to give square-integrable random variables a length and an angle.

The $L^2$ inner product and norm are

$$
\langle X,Y\rangle_{L^2}=E[XY],
\qquad
\|X\|_{L^2}=\sqrt{E[X^2]}.
$$

where

- $X$ and $Y$ are square-integrable random variables.
- $\langle X,Y\rangle_{L^2}$ is the $L^2$ inner product.
- $\|X\|_{L^2}$ is the $L^2$ norm.

Billingsley states that $p=2$ is its own conjugate index, so $(f,g)=\int fg\,d\mu$ is well defined on $L^2$ and $\|f\|^2=(f,f)$. Vershynin writes $\langle X,Y\rangle_{L^2}=E[XY]$ and $\|X\|_{L^2}=(E|X|^2)^{1/2}$. Klenke defines $\langle f,g\rangle=\int fg\,d\mu$ and $\|f\|_2=\langle f,f\rangle^{1/2}$.

Completeness. Every Cauchy sequence in $L^2$ converges in mean square to an element of $L^2$. Thus $L^2$ is a Hilbert space. This principle is used to keep mean-square limits inside the space of square-integrable random variables.

Mean-square completeness says that

$$
\lim_{n,m\to\infty}E[(X_n-X_m)^2]=0
$$

implies the existence of $X\in L^2$ with

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}E[(X_n-X)^2]=0.
$$

where

- $(X_n)$ is a sequence in $L^2$.
- $X$ is the mean-square limit.

Billingsley states that $L^p$ is complete and that $L^2$ is a Hilbert space quite like Euclidean space. Klenke concludes that $(L^2(\mu),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ is a real Hilbert space. Gut states that Cauchy convergence implies convergence in these spaces.

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. For $X,Y\in L^2$ the product $XY$ is integrable and $|E[XY]|$ is at most the product of the $L^2$ norms. This principle is used to justify the inner product and to bound second-moment pairings.

The $L^2$ Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is

$$
|E[XY]|\le\|X\|_{L^2}\,\|Y\|_{L^2}.
$$

where

- $E[XY]$ is the $L^2$ inner product of $X$ and $Y$.
- $\|X\|_{L^2}$ and $\|Y\|_{L^2}$ are the $L^2$ norms.

Kallenberg obtains $|\langle f,g\rangle|\le\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$ from Hölder’s inequality at $p=q=2$. Vershynin states $|E[XY]|\le\|X\|_{L^2}\|Y\|_{L^2}$. Ash and Doléans-Dade record equality when the two vectors are linearly dependent.

Covariance as a centered inner product. After subtracting expectations, the $L^2$ inner product of the centered variables is covariance, and the squared $L^2$ norm of a centered variable is variance. This principle is used to read second-moment statistics as Hilbert-space geometry.

The identifications are

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)
=
\langle X-E[X],\,Y-E[Y]\rangle_{L^2},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(X)
=
\|X-E[X]\|_{L^2}^2.
$$

where

- $X-E[X]$ is the centered version of $X$.
- $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$ is covariance.
- $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ is variance.

Vershynin writes $\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)=\langle X-EX,Y-EY\rangle_{L^2}$ and $\sigma(X)=\|X-EX\|_{L^2}$. Blitzstein takes the length of a random variable to be its standard deviation. Kallenberg records $|\mathrm{cov}(\xi,\eta)|\le\{\mathrm{var}(\xi)\mathrm{var}(\eta)\}^{1/2}$.

Orthogonality of uncorrelated variables. Two centered random variables are orthogonal in $L^2$ precisely when they are uncorrelated. This principle is used to convert vanishing covariance into a Pythagorean decomposition of variance.

Uncorrelated centered variables satisfy

$$
\langle X-E[X],\,Y-E[Y]\rangle_{L^2}=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y).
$$

where

- the first display is orthogonality in $L^2$.
- the second display is additivity of variance.

Billingsley defines orthogonality by a vanishing inner product and records the Pythagorean identity for orthogonal sums. Blitzstein states that two random variables are uncorrelated if and only if they have inner product zero. Klenke states that uncorrelated summands add variances.

Conditional expectation as orthogonal projection. For a sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}$, the space of $\mathcal{G}$-measurable square-integrable random variables is a closed subspace of $L^2$. The conditional expectation $E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto that subspace. This principle is used to identify the mean-square best $\mathcal{G}$-measurable approximation to $X$.

The projection property is

$$
E\bigl[(X-E[X\mid\mathcal{G}])Z\bigr]=0
\quad\text{for all }Z\in L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{G},P).
$$

where

- $\mathcal{G}$ is a sub-$\sigma$-algebra of $\mathcal{F}$.
- $E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is the conditional expectation of $X$ given $\mathcal{G}$.
- $Z$ is a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable square-integrable random variable.

Klenke states that $E[X\mid\mathcal{F}]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ on $L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$. Kallenberg defines $E[\xi\mid\mathcal{F}]$ as the orthogonal Hilbert-space projection onto the $\mathcal{F}$-measurable random variables. Gut writes the integral orthogonality relation that induces the decomposition $X=Y+Z$ with $Y=E(X\mid\mathcal{G})$.
