# Random Variables

A *random variable* is a function, mathematically speaking. They map events in a sample space to a subset of the real numbers. But they can also be considered as sets (see §Convergence of Random Variables, Distributions, and Probabilities.) 

A random variable is typically denoted by $X$ and elements in the domain of the random variable denoted $x$; thus $x \in X$. Along with probability distributions, they are central components in Probability Theory.

Random variable as a function. A random variable is a real-valued function defined on a sample space that assigns a numerical value to each outcome. This principle is used to convert outcomes of a random experiment into numerical quantities that can be analyzed probabilistically.

Realization of a random variable. A random variable is the function defined before the experiment, while a realized value is the number obtained after an outcome occurs. This principle is used to distinguish the probabilistic quantity from a particular observed value.

Distribution of a random variable. The distribution of a random variable specifies the probabilities associated with its possible values through probabilities inherited from the sample space. Discrete random variable. A discrete random variable takes values in a countable set. This principle is used to represent quantities such as counts and categorical outcomes encoded numerically.

Continuous random variable. A continuous random variable has probabilities described by a probability density over intervals of real numbers. This principle is used to represent measurements such as time, distance, mass, and temperature.

Cumulative distribution function. The cumulative distribution function gives the probability that a random variable is less than or equal to a specified real number. This principle is used to characterize the complete distribution of discrete and continuous random variables.

Transformation of a random variable. Applying a function to a random variable produces another random variable. This principle is used to construct derived quantities and determine their probability distributions from known random variables.

## Problems

- Transfer a probability $P$ on a sample space to the law of $X$ on the real line.
- Obtain $P(X\in B)$ from the induced distribution of $X$.
- Separate the random variable $X$ from a realized value $x$.
- Obtain the law of $g(X)$ from the law of $X$.
- Identify a law as discrete, as absolutely continuous, as mixed.
- Recover $P(X\le x)$ from the cumulative distribution function of $X$.
