# Events

A physical occurrence at a definite place and time that is used to label points of spacetime by four coordinates.

Note: Also called a spacetime event. Also called a spacetime point.

<i>

**definition [d]** (*Event*) From Knight: the fundamental element of relativity is called an event. An event is a physical activity that takes place at a definite point in space and at a definite instant of time. We can quantify where and when an event occurs with four numbers: the coordinates $(x, y, z)$ and the instant of time $t$. These four numbers are called the spacetime coordinates of the event.

where

- $(x, y, z)$ are the spatial coordinates of the event.
- $t$ is the time of the event.
- $(x, y, z, t)$ are the spacetime coordinates in one frame.

</i>

<i>

**definition [d]** (*Event*) From Shankar: we begin with the notion of an event. An event is something that happens at a certain place at a certain time. For example, if a firecracker goes off somewhere at some time, the $x$ is where it happened and the $t$ is when it happened. So $(x, t)$ are coordinates in spacetime.

where

- $x$ is the place of the event.
- $t$ is the time of the event.

</i>

<i>

**definition [d]** (*Event = Spacetime Point*) From Carroll: an individual point in spacetime is called an event. Coordinates on spacetime will be denoted by letters with Greek superscript indices running from $0$ to $3$, with $0$ generally denoting the time coordinate. Thus,

- $x^{\mu}:\quad x^{0} = ct,\quad x^{1} = x,\quad x^{2} = y,\quad x^{3} = z$ .

where

- $x^{\mu}$ are the spacetime coordinates of the event.
- $c$ is the speed of light.
- $t$ is the time coordinate.

</i>

<i>

**definition [d]** (*Event*) From Emam: the position $4$-vector contains more information than its three-dimensional counterpart $x^{i}$; it not only tells us the location of an event, it also contains the time when the event happened: $x^{0} = ct$. It is the position of a spacetime point. One can also write

- $x^{\mu} = (x^{0}, x^{i})$

or

- $x^{\mu} = (ct, x, y, z)$

to signify the same thing.

where

- $x^{\mu}$ is the position $4$-vector of the event.
- $x^{i}$ are the spatial components.

</i>

## Elementary Example

### Simple

A firecracker explodes at one place and one time. In a lab frame its spacetime coordinates are

$$
(x, y, z, t) = (2\,\mathrm{m},\, 0,\, 0,\, 1\,\mathrm{s})
$$

where

- this single tuple is one event.

### General

The same event measured in two inertial frames has coordinates $(x,y,z,t)$ in $S$ and $(x',y',z',t')$ in $S'$.

$$
(x,y,z,t)\quad\text{and}\quad (x',y',z',t')
$$

where

- both label the same physical occurrence.
