# Work

Work is the measure of energy transferred between systems.

Note: Also denoted $W$.

<i>

**definition [d]** (*Work*) From Knight: in SECTION 9.3 you learned that a constant force $\mathbf{F}$ does work

- $W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{r} = F\,\Delta r\cos\theta$

as the particle undergoes displacement $\Delta\mathbf{r}$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement.

where

- $W$ is the work.
- $\mathbf{F}$ is the force.
- $\Delta\mathbf{r}$ is the displacement.
- $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{F}$ and $\Delta\mathbf{r}$.

</i>

<i>

**definition [d]** (*Work*) From Shankar: the product $Fd$ is called the work done by the force and denoted by $W$. We have found the simplest version of the work-energy theorem:

- $K_{2} - K_{1} = Fd = W$ .

where

- $W$ is the work done by the force.
- $F$ is the force.
- $d$ is the distance traveled.
- $K_{1}$ and $K_{2}$ are the initial and final kinetic energies.

</i>

<i>

**definition [d]** (*Work*) From Griffiths: the potential difference between points $a$ and $b$ is equal to the work it takes, per unit charge, to carry a particle from $a$ to $b$:

- $V(b) - V(a) = \dfrac{W}{Q}$ ,

so

- $W = Q\bigl(V(b) - V(a)\bigr)$ .

where

- $W$ is the work.
- $Q$ is the test charge.
- $V(a)$ and $V(b)$ are the potentials at $a$ and $b$.

</i>

## Elementary Example

### Simple

For $F = 3$, $\Delta r = 4$, and $\theta = 0$,

$$
W = F\,\Delta r\cos\theta = 12
$$

where

- the force and displacement are parallel.

### General

For $K_{1} = 5$, $K_{2} = 11$, and constant force along the path,

$$
W = K_{2} - K_{1} = 6
$$

where

- work equals the change in kinetic energy.
